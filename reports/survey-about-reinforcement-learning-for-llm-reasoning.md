# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Methods, Evidence, and Open Problems

## TL;DR
- RL for reasoning extends preference-based post-training by using signals tied to answer correctness, intermediate steps, or both; the choice of reward granularity changes what the system is encouraged to get right. [1][2][3]
- Verifiable outcome rewards offer an attractive path when answers can be checked, while process and hybrid rewards try to address sparse feedback or expose errors hidden by a correct final answer. [3][4][5]
- Recent work explores data-efficient and prolonged RL, verifier-free rewards, joint policy–reward optimization, and updated sequence-level optimization; these are promising directions, not settled general solutions. [6][7][8][9][10]
- The empirical picture is sensitive to evaluation design: budget mismatch, attempt inflation, calibration drift, and contamination can alter apparent gains, although other analyses report improvements in correctly reasoned solutions. [11][12]

## Background
In this survey, reasoning means generating intermediate steps that contribute to solving a task, rather than producing only a final answer. Chain-of-thought prompting established this operational framing and found gains on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic tasks for sufficiently large models. [13] RL enters the picture as a way to optimize model behavior against feedback: the general RLHF recipe collects demonstrations, trains a reward model on human rankings, and uses PPO to optimize that reward while constraining drift from the supervised model. [1] That recipe is not reasoning-specific; later work makes the reward target more directly about correct answers or sound intermediate reasoning. [2][3]

It is important to distinguish RL training from related reasoning techniques. STaR bootstraps rationales by generating, filtering, and fine-tuning on successful reasoning examples, whereas Tree of Thoughts searches over candidate intermediate steps at inference time; neither is itself an RL training method. [14][15] They nevertheless clarify the broader design space: systems may improve reasoning by learning from traces, by searching among them, or by using feedback to update a policy.

## Reward granularity: outcomes, processes, and hybrids
Outcome supervision evaluates a final answer, while process supervision evaluates intermediate steps. [2][3] Outcome feedback can require less label supervision and may be adequate when correctness is easy to verify, but it does not directly distinguish a valid derivation from an incorrect trace that happens to reach the right answer. [2][12] Process feedback can target that distinction: on MATH, a process-supervised model solved 78.2% of a representative test subset, and PRM800K provides 800,000 step-level human feedback labels. [3] A separate GSM8K study reported a combined supervised-learning and reward-model RL approach reducing final-answer error from 16.8% to 12.7%, and reasoning-trace error among final-answer-correct solutions from 14.0% to 3.4%. [2]

Recent methods extend this spectrum. P2S calculates path-faithfulness rewards from conditional probabilities to provide fine-grained process reward in general-domain reasoning; VPRMs instead apply deterministic, rule-based checks to intermediate steps in medical evidence synthesis. [4][16] PPR combines step-level assessment with outcome verification for non-verifiable agentic tasks, whereas RLPR uses token-probability scores as a reward without an external verifier. [5][6] These designs differ in the evidence they trust: human step labels, deterministic checks, learned probability signals, or final-answer verification. Their abstracts report improvements, but the notes available here do not provide comparable effect sizes across methods. [4][16][5][6]

Reward design remains a practical risk. A study of reward design reports that popular reward models do not always improve RL training, and that refinement intended to prevent reward hacking can make reward models useful. [17] Confidence-weighted clipping is another proposed adjustment: ConfClip combines verifiable outcome rewards with model-confidence estimates and reports improved performance and lower inference-token use, without quantitative effect sizes in the source summary. [18] Together, these results suggest that reward choice and reward handling are part of the method, not neutral implementation details. [18][17]

## Policy learning, efficiency, and optimization
The base policy update still matters even when a reward is available. Recent research explores RLVR with only one training example and reports math-reasoning gains, while ProRL studies prolonged RL and reports novel strategies and improvements over base models. [7][8] These results point to two different efficiency questions—how little task-specific data is needed and how long optimization can continue—but do not establish that either approach transfers uniformly across tasks. [7][8]

Other work changes the optimization setup itself. Cooper jointly optimizes policy and reward models, with robustness and reward-hacking mitigation as goals. [9] ReSPO addresses gradient starvation in clipped policy optimization: its analysis attributes suppression of under-generated positive responses and dominance by over-generated negative responses to sign-dependent clipping effects, with rollout reuse increasing mismatch between the current and data-generating policies. It proposes a smooth two-branch sequence-level reshaping method in place of clipping. [10] These efforts reflect a shift from asking only which reward to optimize toward asking how reward models and policy updates interact over repeated rollouts. [10][9]

## Evaluation and strength of evidence
Reasoning evaluation must distinguish a correct answer from a correct derivation. CoT-Pass@K was proposed to count success only when both the final answer and intermediate reasoning are correct; its authors report that RLVR can extend reasoning performance boundaries on math and coding tasks and incentivize correct reasoning early in GRPO-style training. [12] This is a substantive counterpoint to the idea that outcome rewards necessarily teach only answer production, but it depends on the evaluation criterion and the tasks studied. [12]

Other work warns that headline improvements can be confounded. A study of RLVR measurement gaps identifies unmatched evaluation budgets, attempt inflation and calibration drift, and benchmark contamination; budget-matched reproductions and partial-prompt contamination probes found that several reported gaps shrank substantially or disappeared. [11] It recommends budget-matched saturation curves, variance reporting, calibration and abstention tracking, judge stress tests, and contamination screening. [11] Complementary benchmark analysis argues that test-set unseen-ness alone may not diagnose generalization: it introduces Oracle Performance Gap and reports nearly identical performance for a model trained on a benchmark's training split and an oracle trained on its test split, while stress tests expose weaknesses across difficulty, shifts, and counterfactuals. [19]

Contamination detection is also changing. Self-Critique uses post-RL output-entropy patterns to detect RL-stage contamination; the associated RL-MIA benchmark simulates such contamination, and the paper reports up to a 30% AUC improvement while existing methods are near random-guess performance on RL-phase contamination. [20] The reported evidence therefore supports a cautious evaluation practice: test not only final accuracy, but also reasoning validity, resource budgets, calibration, distributional robustness, and possible training-set exposure. [11][12][19][20]

## Trends and open problems
Across 2024–2026, the field has moved beyond a simple contrast between human preference rewards and final-answer checks. Recent work studies fine-grained probabilistic or verifiable process feedback, hybrid rewards, verifier-free reward signals, confidence-aware reward shaping, and joint policy–reward learning. [4][16][5][6][18][9] In parallel, one-example and prolonged-RL studies probe data efficiency and training duration, and sequence-level optimization work targets off-policy mismatch and gradient starvation. [10][7][8]

Important questions remain open. The 2025 survey identifies scalability and infrastructure as challenges; reward hacking is studied explicitly in a testbed that separates proxy test-harness rewards from mathematical correctness. [21][22] It remains difficult to establish when gains reflect better reasoning rather than additional attempts, benchmark familiarity, or a reward proxy that can be gamed. [11][19][22] Evaluation standards that align budgets and test conditions, score reasoning as well as answers, and probe contamination and reward hacking are therefore central to judging progress. [11][12][20][22]
