# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: A Survey

## TL;DR
- “Small” and “efficient” cover complementary levers: designing or compressing a compact model changes its parameter footprint, while runtime methods reduce memory movement, cache pressure, or scheduling overhead. Which lever helps depends on the workload bottleneck. [1][2][3]
- Distillation, pruning, and quantization are not interchangeable: they transfer behavior, remove weights, and lower numeric precision, respectively; reported gains depend on architecture, hardware, and evaluation setting. [2][4][5][6]
- Serving innovations can raise throughput without shrinking the model, but benefits are workload-dependent; KV-cache techniques help most when memory is the constraint, and some speculative-decoding evidence remains modeled rather than empirically validated. [7][3][8]
- Recent work increasingly treats compact models as components in routed, sparse, or collaborative systems, while new evaluations highlight that benchmark rankings and robustness may not predict deployment behavior. [9][10][11][12][13]

## Background
Small language models (SLMs) are framed as useful for language tasks under limited computational resources, including mobile, on-device, and edge settings. The field therefore includes more than parameter count: architecture and training choices interact with compression, inference software, target hardware, and task-specific evaluation. [1] A broad compression taxonomy includes pruning, quantization, low-rank decomposition, knowledge distillation, and lightweight model design, with different mechanisms and implementation tradeoffs. [2]

## Compression and compact model design
The main compression families act at different points. Distillation trains a smaller student to reproduce a larger teacher; pruning removes weights or structures; quantization uses lower-precision representations; and low-rank or lightweight designs change how the model is represented or constructed. Structured pruning removes units such as channels, unlike unstructured removal of individual weights, and may avoid reliance on sparse-kernel support. Post-training quantization (PTQ) calibrates a pretrained model without retraining, whereas quantization-aware training incorporates quantization during fine-tuning. [2]

Classic compact-model results illustrate why the method and metric matter. DistilBERT transfers information during pretraining and reports 40% fewer parameters than BERT, 97% retained language-understanding performance, and 60% faster inference. [4] MobileBERT instead combines a deep, thin bottleneck architecture with knowledge transfer; the paper reports 4.3× smaller size and 5.5× faster inference than BERT_BASE, alongside a GLUE score of 77.7 and 62 ms latency on Pixel 4. These are not directly interchangeable comparisons because the models and evaluation settings differ. [14]

Methods developed for larger models show further distinctions. Wanda uses weight magnitude multiplied by the corresponding input-activation norm to select unstructured weights for removal, without retraining or weight updates; its paper reports matching dense-model mean zero-shot accuracy at 50% sparsity for LLaMA-65B and LLaMA-2-70B. [5] GPTQ is instead a one-shot, approximate-second-order PTQ method: it reports 3–4-bit weights for OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B with minimal perplexity increase, and speedups attributed to reduced memory movement rather than less multiplication. [6] Together these examples caution against treating a smaller checkpoint, a sparse checkpoint, and a low-bit checkpoint as equivalent forms of efficiency: hardware support and the inference bottleneck determine whether nominal compression becomes lower latency or higher throughput. [2][5][6]

## Runtime efficiency: memory, scheduling, and speculative decoding
Inference-time optimization can improve a model’s serving behavior without changing its learned capabilities. PagedAttention manages KV cache in non-contiguous blocks and supports sharing; the vLLM evaluation reports 2–4× serving-throughput gains over compared systems at similar latency, with larger gains in longer-sequence and more complex-decoding settings. Its advantage is weaker for short, compute-bound workloads, consistent with memory pressure being the relevant bottleneck. [3] A different design, vAttention, uses system demand paging and aims to avoid changes to attention kernels or serving frameworks. [15]

Scheduling addresses a separate source of latency. Sarathi-Serve chunks prefill and schedules it alongside ongoing decoding, limiting work per iteration to avoid prefill-induced stalls while retaining larger batches. The paper reports higher serving capacity across several tested configurations, but its figures depend on model, hardware, and latency target. [8] FlashAttention-2 improves exact attention through IO-aware work partitioning and reports faster attention in its benchmarks; its cited end-to-end model measurements are for training, so they should not be read as serving-throughput evidence. [16]

Speculative decoding trades extra draft work for the chance to accept multiple target tokens. QuantSpec combines self-speculation with hierarchical 4-bit KV-cache and weight quantization, reporting acceptance above 90%, speedups up to about 2.5×, and lower memory use in its long-context edge setting. [17] SPIRe proposes a sparse draft model for large-batch, medium-to-long-context use; its reported throughput improvements come from a performance model, and the paper calls for empirical validation. [7] Compatibility itself is a concern: a Hugging Face paper summary reports that tree-style verification can become expensive with 4-bit models and describes a hierarchical framework with a reported 2.78× speedup. [18] Thus, speculative methods cannot be evaluated by draft acceptance alone; verification cost, quantization, cache footprint, batch size, context length, and the target SLO all matter. [7][17][18]

## Capability, routing, and evaluation of small models
Compression alone does not explain what capabilities a compact model retains or acquires. A recent controlled study separates factual acquisition from compositional skill in on-policy distillation and reports that reverse-KL distillation transferred compositional skill to unseen reasoning structures more reliably than factual knowledge. [19] In a different setting, SpatialOPSD explores internalizing spatial capabilities from summarized, verified agent traces to avoid inference-time tools and their external dependencies. [20] These results suggest that data and distillation objectives can specialize behavior, but they do not establish that any single recipe generalizes across tasks.

SLMs can also operate as part of a larger system. SWARM-LLM routes queries between local small models and a cloud foundation model using uncertainty estimates and safety signals. [11] At finer granularity, TokenRouter studies token-level model routing; its summary describes potential cost-quality benefits while identifying step desynchronization and frequent batch admission as serving challenges. [9] These approaches shift the comparison from “small versus large” to when and how to invoke each model, with system complexity and routing decisions becoming part of the efficiency question. [9][11]

Evaluation needs to reflect the intended deployment. A recent safety-benchmark analysis reports ambiguous judgments for SLMs, a capability-safety confound, and brittle rankings. [12] For multimodal small models in industrial anomaly inspection, RobustMAD reports capabilities alongside gaps in grounding, completeness, and logical reasoning. [13] Predictive spatial reasoning benchmarks also test anticipation of unseen outcomes rather than only recognition of spatial relations already visible in the prompt; SpaceCast-Bench reports 3,862 questions from 182 scenes across 16 task types. [21] These examples argue for evaluating robustness, task scope, and operational constraints—not just aggregate benchmark scores—before drawing deployment conclusions. [21][12][13]

## Trends and open problems
The recent direction is from isolated model compression toward coordinated inference systems. Work in 2025–2026 explores quantized speculative decoding, token-level routing, sparse-first serving for long agent histories, and local/cloud collaboration. [9][10][11][18] SparseEngine, for example, is described as coordinating KV representation and computation with common serving lifecycle transitions, while targeting the memory and attention demands of long agent histories. [10] The common theme is managing the whole inference path—model, cache, scheduler, and routing—rather than optimizing parameter count alone. [9][10][11]

Several issues remain open in the evidence reviewed here. First, gains are conditional: cache management helps memory-bound workloads, chunked scheduling exposes throughput/latency tradeoffs, and speculative decoding depends on the cost of verifying drafts. [7][3][8][18] Second, reported numbers are not always comparable: for example, SPIRe’s throughput claim is model-based, FlashAttention-2’s cited end-to-end evidence is training-oriented, and SLM safety rankings can be brittle. [7][16][12] Finally, local/cloud systems and compact multimodal models need deployment-aware tests for safety, grounding, and robustness; summaries of recent work identify these as active concerns rather than settled properties. [11][12][13]
